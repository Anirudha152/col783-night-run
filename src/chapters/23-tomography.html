Tomography: images from projections

Pre-minorDeck 12

CT measures line integrals

X-rays lose energy passing through tissue with absorption \(\mu(x,y)\):

\[ I = I_0\exp\!\Big(-\int\mu\,dl\Big) \;\Rightarrow\; -\ln\frac{I}{I_0} = \int\mu(x,y)\,dl \quad(\text{a raysum}) \]

The Radon transform collects raysums along every line \(x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta = \rho\):

\[ g_\theta(\rho) = \iint f(x,y)\,\delta(x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta - \rho)\,dx\,dy \]

Backprojection

Smear each projection back across the image along its rays, then add over all angles:

\[ f_\theta(x,y) = g_\theta(x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta), \qquad \hat f(x,y) = \int_0^\pi f_\theta(x,y)\,d\theta \]

This "laminogram" is blurry: every point becomes a star of lines, i.e. a halo that falls off like \(1/r\). More angles help but never remove the blur.

Projection-slice theorem

\[ G_\theta(\omega) = F(\omega\cos\theta,\ \omega\sin\theta) \]

The 1D Fourier transform of one projection = one line (slice) through the 2D Fourier transform of the image, at the same angle.

Proof for θ = 0: \(g_0(\rho) = \int f(\rho, y)\,dy\). Its transform \(G_0(\omega) = \iint f(\rho,y)e^{-2\pi i\omega\rho}\,d\rho\,dy = F(\omega, 0)\). Rotation does the rest.

Filtered backprojection

The slices fill the 2D frequency plane, but crowd near the centre: density ∝ \(1/|\omega|\). Plain backprojection over-weights low frequencies → blur. Fix: weight each slice by \(|\omega|\) first.

  1. For each θ: \(G_\theta = \mathcal F(g_\theta)\).
  2. Multiply by the ramp \(|\omega|\) (times a smooth window).
  3. Inverse transform → filtered projection \(\tilde g_\theta\).
  4. Backproject \(\tilde g_\theta\) and add.
  • \(|\omega|\) alone is not square-integrable and boosts noise; a hard cutoff rings.
  • Use a smooth window, e.g. \(c + (1-c)\cos(2\pi\omega/M)\), \(c = 0.54\) (Hamming).
  • In practice: convolve each \(g_\theta\) with \(\mathcal F^{-1}(H(\omega)|\omega|)\).

Exam-style practice

What is the Radon transform of a single point \(\delta(x - x_0, y - y_0)\)?
Answer

\(g_\theta(\rho) = \delta(\rho - (x_0\cos\theta + y_0\sin\theta))\). For each θ a spike at \(\rho = x_0\cos\theta + y_0\sin\theta\): a sinusoid in the sinogram. (Same curve as in the Hough transform.)

Why is simple backprojection blurry, and what does the ramp filter fix?
Answer

By projection-slice, backprojecting adds Fourier slices through the origin. Low frequencies get contributions from every slice; high frequencies from few. So low frequencies are over-weighted by \(1/|\omega|\) → blur. Multiplying by \(|\omega|\) evens it out.

Cheat sheet · tomography

  • Raysum \(-\ln(I/I_0) = \int\mu\,dl\)
  • Radon \(g_\theta(\rho) = \iint f\,\delta(x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta-\rho)\)
  • Disk: \(2A\sqrt{r^2-\rho^2}\); point → sinusoid
  • Backproj. \(\hat f = \int_0^\pi g_\theta(x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta)\,d\theta\): 1/r blur
  • Proj-slice \(G_\theta(\omega) = F(\omega\cos\theta,\omega\sin\theta)\)
  • FBP: \(|\omega|\) × window (Hamming c = 0.54), then backproject
